Stock Valuation & Equity Basics

A share is worth the present value of the dividends it will pay — everything else is a shortcut to that number. Voting rights decide who controls the firm; multiples are a sanity check, not a valuation.

Key ideas
  • Price today = PV of expected dividends: P0 = Div1/(1+rE) + P1/(1+rE) for one period; with constant growth P0 = Div1/(rE − g); with zero growth (preferred stock) P0 = Div/rE.
  • The equity cost of capital splits into two pieces: rE = dividend yield (Div1/P0) + capital-gain rate (g). Under constant growth the price grows at the same rate g as the dividend.
  • Growth comes from reinvesting earnings: g = retention rate x return on new investment. Dividend = EPS x payout ratio, and retention = 1 − payout.
  • Cutting dividends to reinvest raises the price only if the new projects earn more than rE (positive NPV); if return on new investment = rE, growth is worthless and P0 = EPS1/rE.
  • Comparables: P0 = EPS x peer P/E; EV = EBITDA x peer EV/EBITDA, then Equity = EV − Debt + Cash, Price = Equity / shares. Forward P/E = payout/(rE − g) links multiples back to the DDM.
  • Markets are informationally efficient when public news is impossible to profit from: the price moves immediately to the new DDM value, so you sell at the new price, not the old one. Cumulative voting lets a minority holder win proportional board seats; straight voting lets a majority win every seat; dual-class shares separate votes from ownership.

Exam 2 practice Q3One-period DDM: solve for the future price

Evco stock sells for $53.39 today and will pay a $1.95 dividend in one year; its equity cost of capital is 13%. What price must you expect Evco to sell for immediately after paying the dividend in one year to justify today's price? (Round to the nearest cent.)

GivenP0 = $53.39; Div1 = $1.95; rE = 13% = 0.13
  1. 1. Solve for P1 in the one-period model: P0 = (Div1 + P1)/(1 + rE).
  2. 2. Rearrange: P1 = P0 (1 + rE) − Div1.
  3. 3. Substitute: P1 = 53.39 x 1.13 − 1.95 = 60.3307 − 1.95 = 58.3807.
AnswerP1 = $58.38

TrapStudents compound the price and forget to subtract the dividend (getting $60.33), or divide instead of multiplying by 1.13. The future price is ex-dividend: the $1.95 is paid out, so it comes off the total. Matches the key ($58.38).

Exam 2 practice Q10Dividend cut to fund expansion: back out rE, then re-value

Cooperton Mining just announced it will cut its dividend from $4.00 to $2.50 per share and use the extra funds to expand. Before the announcement its dividends were expected to grow at 3.0% and the share price was $50.00. With the expansion, dividends are expected to grow at 5.0%. (a) What share price do you expect after the announcement, assuming the expansion does not change risk? (nearest cent) (b) Is the expansion a good investment?

GivenOld Div1 = $4.00, old g = 3.0%, old P0 = $50.00; new Div1 = $2.50, new g = 5.0%; rE unchanged
  1. 1. Back out the equity cost of capital from the old price: rE = Div1/P0 + g = 4.00/50.00 + 0.03 = 0.08 + 0.03 = 0.11 = 11%.
  2. 2. Re-value with the new dividend and growth: P0 = 2.50/(0.11 − 0.05) = 2.50/0.06 = 41.67.
  3. 3. (b) The price falls from $50.00 to $41.67, so the expansion destroys value: it is not a good investment (the extra growth does not compensate for the cash given up, i.e. the projects' return is below rE).
Answer(a) New price = $41.67 (b) It is NOT a good investment

TrapThere is no rE in the problem; you must recover it from the old price (rE = 11%) before using the new numbers. Plugging the new growth into the old dividend or forgetting that both Div1 and g change gives the wrong price. Matches the key ($41.67, not a good investment).
Exam 2 practice Q11Changing-growth DDM (three explicit dividends, then constant growth)

CX Enterprises expects dividends of $1.01 in one year, $1.23 in two years, and $1.33 in three years. After that, dividends grow at 4.1% per year forever (year 4's dividend is 4.1% more than $1.33). If rE = 12.3%, what is the current stock price? (nearest cent)

  1. 1. Terminal (year-3) value of the constant-growth dividends: Div4 = 1.33 x 1.041 = 1.38453; P3 = Div4/(rE − g) = 1.38453/(0.123 − 0.041) = 1.38453/0.082 = 16.8845.
  2. 2. Discount factors: 1.123 = 1.123; 1.123^2 = 1.26113; 1.123^3 = 1.41625.
  3. 3. PV of explicit dividends: 1.01/1.123 = 0.8994; 1.23/1.26113 = 0.9753; 1.33/1.41625 = 0.9391.
  4. 4. PV of terminal value: 16.8845/1.41625 = 11.9220.
  5. 5. Sum: 0.8994 + 0.9753 + 0.9391 + 11.9220 = 14.7358.

Exam 2 practice Q26Implied growth rate from a P/E ratio

SLYMN Enterprises has a P/E ratio of 12.00 and a dividend payout ratio of 40%. If its equity cost of capital is 13.00%, what growth rate is its P/E ratio consistent with? (two decimal places)

GivenForward P/E = 12.00; payout ratio = 40% = 0.40; rE = 13% = 0.13
  1. 1. Forward P/E from the DDM: P0/EPS1 = (Div1/EPS1)/(rE − g) = payout/(rE − g).
  2. 2. Substitute: 12.00 = 0.40/(0.13 − g).
  3. 3. Solve: 0.13 − g = 0.40/12.00 = 0.033333, so g = 0.13 − 0.033333 = 0.096667.
Answerg = 9.67%
TI-84.13-.4/12
TrapStudents forget the payout ratio and write 12 = 1/(rE − g), getting g = 4.67%. The P/E links price to EARNINGS, but the DDM needs the DIVIDEND, so the payout ratio must appear in the numerator. Matches the key (9.67%).
Traps
  • Div1 vs Div0: the Gordon model needs NEXT period's dividend. If a problem says the firm 'just paid' or 'currently pays' a dividend, grow it one period first (Div1 = Div0 (1+g)); if it says 'will pay in one year', use it as is.
  • Period matching: a quarterly dividend needs a quarterly rate. Convert an effective annual rE with (1 + rE)^(1/4) − 1, not rE/4.
  • Changing growth: the terminal value Div(N+1)/(rE − g) is a value at year N, so discount it N periods, and build Div(N+1) by growing the last listed dividend once.
  • Any change in payout changes BOTH the dividend and g = retention x return on new investment; re-compute both before re-pricing. Higher payout only raises the price when the reinvested projects earn less than rE.
  • When the price and growth are given but rE is not, back rE out from the old price (rE = Div1/P0 + g) before re-valuing after an announcement.
  • Enterprise multiples value the whole firm: after EV = multiple x EBITDA, subtract debt and add cash before dividing by shares. P/E already gives an equity price per share; do not adjust it for debt.
  • Pick the comparable that is actually comparable in business and growth (Coca-Cola for PepsiCo, not a tiny craft-soda maker with a 35x P/E). When leverage differs across comparables, EV/EBITDA is more reliable than P/E.
  • The P/E-to-DDM bridge is P/E = payout/(rE − g); forgetting the payout ratio is the most common error when backing out g.

Chapter Eleven

Risk and Return

Returns are random, so this chapter is about measuring them honestly: realized versus expected, arithmetic versus geometric, and how much of a single stock's volatility you can diversify away for free.

Key ideas
  • Realized return = dividend yield + capital gain: R = (Div1 + P1 − P0) / P0; multi-period returns compound multiplicatively, not additively.
  • Geometric (compound) average is what you actually earned; arithmetic average is the unbiased estimate of NEXT period's return. Geometric ≤ arithmetic, always.
  • Volatility is measured by SD = √(Var), with the variance denominator T − 1 for a sample of realized returns; the 95% prediction interval is R_avg +/- 2 SD and the precision of the average is SE = SD / √(T).
  • Independent risks diversify: for n independent exposures the SD of the total grows as √(n) but the SD of the average falls as 1 / √(n).
  • Higher volatility -> higher expected return holds for large, well-diversified portfolios (their volatility is systematic risk) but NOT for individual stocks, whose volatility includes diversifiable firm-specific risk that investors are not paid to bear. For individual stocks, use β (Ch. 12).
  • Realized return over one period = (Div + P_end − P_begin) / P_begin, which splits exactly into dividend yield (Div/P_begin) plus capital gain rate ((P_end − P_begin)/P_begin).
  • Multi-period realized return compounds: 1 + R = (1+R1)(1+R2)...(1+RT); each sub-period return uses that sub-period's beginning price as the base and the dividend paid in that sub-period.

Is it true that higher volatility means higher expected return?

Arithmetic average estimates the NEXT period's return; geometric (compound) average is what you actually earned over the history. Geometric is always ≤ arithmetic.

The SD of the total grows with √(n), but the SD of the average per exposure falls as SD(one) / √(n), so independent risks diversify away as the pool grows.

Chapter Twelve

Systematic Risk and the CAPM

Only systematic risk earns a premium. Beta measures it and the CAPM prices it — E[R] = r_f + β × MRP — with the market portfolio as the benchmark.

Key ideas
  • Portfolio return is the value-weighted average of holding returns using BEGINNING-of-period weights; it always lies between the best and worst holding.
  • Diversification: a two-stock portfolio's SD is at most the weighted average of the two SDs, and equals it only when Corr = +1. Lower correlation means more risk eliminated.
  • Firm-specific risk can be diversified away for free, so investors are not paid to bear it. Only systematic risk earns a premium, and β is the measure of it.
  • Beta = Cov(R_i, R_mkt) / Var(R_mkt) = Corr x (SD_i / SD_mkt). A stock's excess return over r_f is β times the market's excess return.
  • CAPM: E[R_i] = r_f + β_i x MRP, where MRP = E[R_mkt] − r_f. If the problem gives the market RETURN, subtract r_f first to get the premium.
  • Portfolio β is the weighted average of the holdings' betas: β_P = sum of w_i x β_i.
  • Portfolio weight x_i = (shares x price of stock i) / total portfolio value; weights always sum to 100%. Portfolio return R_P = sum of x_i R_i, where the weights are the beginning-of-period (pre-move) weights.

Traps
  • Market return vs. market risk premium: if the problem gives the market RETURN, subtract r_f before multiplying by β. MRP = E[R_mkt] − r_f.
  • Excess return and expected return are different answers: excess = β x MRP; expected = r_f + β x MRP. Read which one is asked.
  • Portfolio weights use BEGINNING-of-period values (price x shares before the move), not ending values.
  • Quick check: a portfolio return must lie between the best and worst holding's return. If it does not, a weight is wrong.
  • Two-stock SD: do not just average the SDs. Compute variance with the 2 w_1 w_2 Corr SD_1 SD_2 cross term, then take the square root. The weighted-average SD is only correct when Corr = +1.
  • Keep units consistent inside the variance formula: either all SDs as decimals or all as percents; squaring a percent gives percent-squared.
  • Beta uses Var(R_mkt) = SD_mkt^2 in the denominator, not SD_mkt. The correlation form Corr x SD_i / SD_mkt avoids this.
  • High volatility does not mean high expected return for an individual stock; only β (systematic risk) is priced.
Concept checks
Is it true that higher volatility means higher expected return?
True for a large, well-diversified portfolio, whose volatility is all systematic risk. Not true for individual stocks: a single stock's volatility includes firm-specific risk that investors can diversify away for free and are not paid to bear. For individual stocks, use β, not volatility.
How do you compute a stock's excess return from its beta?
Excess return = β x market risk premium. If you are given the market return rather than the premium, subtract r_f first. Expected return = r_f + β x MRP. Example: r_f 3.5%, market 10%, β 1.3 gives MRP 6.5%, excess 8.45%, E[R] 11.95%.
When does diversification between two stocks eliminate the most risk?
When their correlation is lowest. Portfolio SD is at most the weighted average of the two SDs, and reaches that upper bound only at Corr = +1; any lower correlation makes SD_P smaller than the weighted average.
Which weights go into a portfolio return calculation?
Beginning-of-period value weights (value of each holding divided by total value at the start). After a price move, new weights are w_i,old (1 + R_i) / (1 + R_P).

Chapter Thirteen

Cost of Capital

A firm's cost of capital is the market-value-weighted return its investors require. WACC is the discount rate for projects of the firm's average risk — and the tax shield on debt is already inside it.

Key ideas
  • Weights are MARKET values: E = price × shares, MV of debt = par × (% of par), V = E + P + D. Never use book value, par, or retained earnings.
  • WACC = (E/V) r_E + (P/V) r_P + (D/V) r_D (1 − τ_C). Only the debt term gets (1 − τ_C); preferred and equity get no tax adjustment.
  • Cost of equity comes from CAPM (r_E = r_f + β × MRP) or the constant dividend growth model (r_E = Div_1/P_0 + g); cost of preferred is Div_pfd / Price_pfd; cost of debt is the bond's YTM (at par, coupon rate = YTM).
  • Enterprise-value form uses Net Debt = Debt − Cash and weights MVE/EV and NetDebt/EV.
  • Project value: V_0 = FCF_1 / (r_WACC − g); NPV = −I_0 + V_0 − issuance costs. FCF = EBIT(1 − τ_C) + Dep − CapEx − change in NWC.
  • Bond YTM on the calculator is semi-annual: N = 2 × years, PMT = coupon/2, FV = 100, then multiply I/Y by 2.
  • Weights are MARKET values, never book: E = shares × price, D = par × (% of par), P = pref shares × pref price. Book equity and retained earnings are distractors.

13.1 A First Look at the Weighted Average Cost of Capital

  • A firm's capital structure is the relative proportion of each financing source (equity, debt, preferred) outstanding; a levered firm uses debt, while an unlevered firm is financed 100% with equity.
  • The market-value balance sheet identity is V = E + D + P: total firm value equals the market value of equity plus debt plus preferred stock.
  • WACC weights must be based on market values, not book values, because market values reflect investors' current opportunity cost and are forward-looking, whereas book values are backward-looking.
  • The basic WACC formula (Eq. 13.2) is rWACC = (E/V)rE + (D/V)rD(1 − tc), where the tax shield applies only to debt.
  • Capital structures vary widely across firms: in 2022 Tesla was 99% equity / 1% debt, Amazon 88% / 12%, and Verizon 53% / 47% (Figure 13.2, market-value weights).

13.2 The Firm's Costs of Debt and Equity Capital

  • The effective (after-tax) cost of debt is rD x (1 − tc) (Eq. 13.3), where rD is the pretax yield to maturity; interest is tax-deductible so the government effectively subsidizes debt financing.
  • The cost of preferred stock is rP = DivPfd / PricePfd (Eq. 13.4) with no tax adjustment, because preferred dividends are paid from after-tax income and are not deductible.
  • Method 1 for the cost of common equity is the CAPM (Eq. 13.5): rE = rf + β x (E[Rmkt] − rf); it requires a β estimate, a risk-free rate, and a market risk premium, and works for any publicly traded stock with a β.
  • Method 2 is the constant dividend growth model (CDGM): rE = Div1/P0 + g; it assumes the dividend estimate is correct and growth is constant, so it suits stable, dividend-paying stocks.
  • Best practice is to calculate both CAPM and CDGM: if the results are close, average them; if very different, examine which set of assumptions is more realistic, or solve for the growth rate g that would make the two methods agree.

13.3 A Second Look at the Weighted Average Cost of Capital

  • The full WACC formula (Eq. 13.6) adds a preferred stock tranche: rWACC = (E/V)rE + (P/V)rP + (D/V)rD(1 − tc).
  • Firms in the same economy can have different WACCs because they differ in leverage and cost of equity; a higher β implies a higher cost of equity and therefore a higher WACC (Figure 13.3).
  • To estimate the market risk premium, the S&P 500's historical excess return over 1-year T-bills was 7.7% (1926-2021) and 5.0% (1965-2021); over 10-year Treasury notes it was 5.9% and 3.9% respectively (Table 13.2), so a longer sample period gives a higher premium.
  • In practice most firms use the yield on long-term Treasury bonds (not T-bills) as the risk-free rate and a market risk premium in the 5%-7% range; a 1% difference in the premium can materially change a project's NPV and accept/reject decision.
  • The enterprise value approach (Eq. 13.8), rWACC = rE(MVE/EV) + rD(1 − tc)(NetDebt/EV), nets cash against debt and is equivalent to Eq. 13.6; it is widely used in practice.

13.4 Using the WACC to Value a Project

  • The WACC method values a project by discounting its expected free cash flows at the firm's WACC; for a growing perpetuity, the levered value is V0L = FCF1 / (rWACC − g) (Eq. 13.9), and discounting at the WACC incorporates the tax benefit of leverage.
  • Assumption 1 (average risk): the project's market risk equals the average market risk of the firm's investments, which holds for typical projects in the firm's main business and expansions of existing operations.
  • Assumption 2 (constant D/E ratio): the firm continuously adjusts leverage to maintain a constant ratio of the market value of debt to equity, which is reasonable because firms tend to add debt as they grow.
  • Assumption 3 (limited leverage effects): the main effect of leverage on value is the interest tax deduction, with other factors insignificant at the firm's debt level; this holds for moderate debt levels but not in financial distress.
  • A project is accepted when its NPV at the WACC is positive, meaning it earns more than the firm's cost of capital and creates value for shareholders (e.g., the AT&T facility extension with NPV of $44.95M at a 5.3% WACC).

13.5 Project-Based Costs of Capital

  • The core principle is to use the cost of capital appropriate to the specific project, not the firm's overall WACC, unless the project has the same risk as the firm's typical investments.
  • For a project in the firm's same line of business, use the firm's WACC; for a new division in a different business, use a comparable pure-play company's WACC; for an acquisition, use the target's WACC.
  • Using the wrong rate leads to incorrect accept/reject decisions: AT&T's WACC is 5.3% while Netflix's is 9.7%, so AT&T evaluating a streaming business at 5.3% would understate its risk.
  • A comparable company's cost of capital is obtained by applying the CAPM to the comparable's β; if the comparable has no debt, its WACC equals its cost of equity.
  • Discounting a riskier project at the firm's lower WACC overstates value and risks accepting negative-NPV projects.

13.6 When Raising External Capital Is Costly

  • Raising external capital incurs direct costs: underwriting fees paid to investment banks (typically 3%-7% of equity raised), legal and accounting costs (vary by deal size), registration fees to the SEC and exchanges (relatively small), and underpricing of new equity (often the largest hidden cost).
  • Issuance costs are treated as additional negative cash flows at time 0 and subtracted from the project's NPV: Adjusted NPV = Project NPV − Issuance Costs.
  • Issuance costs are real cash outflows and can turn a marginally positive NPV negative, so they must be included whenever a project requires external financing.

Concept checks
MACRS vs. straight-line depreciation: which is better for a project and why?
Total depreciation and total tax savings are identical under both. MACRS front-loads the deductions, so the depreciation tax shield (Dep × τ) arrives earlier and has a higher PV, giving a higher NPV today. Always prefer the accelerated method for cash-flow purposes. Straight-line only 'wins' for smoother reported earnings — bookkeeping, not cash.
Why does only the debt term in WACC get multiplied by (1 − tau_C)?
Interest on debt is tax-deductible, creating an interest tax shield; dividends on preferred and common equity are not deductible, so r_P and r_E enter WACC with no tax adjustment.
Why must WACC weights be market values rather than book values?
The cost of capital reflects what investors currently require on the market value of their claims. Book equity, par value, and retained earnings are historical accounting figures and do not measure what the capital is worth today.
How do you get the cost of equity from beta when given the market return instead of the market risk premium?
Subtract r_f first to get MRP = market return − r_f, then excess return = β × MRP and r_E = r_f + β × MRP. Example: r_f 3.5%, market 10%, β 1.3 → MRP 6.5%, excess 8.45%, r_E 11.95%.
How is the pretax cost of debt found when a bond is not trading at par?
Compute its YTM: N = 2 × years, PMT = (coupon rate × 100)/2, PV = −price, FV = 100, CPT I/Y, then multiply by 2. Then apply (1 − τ_C) for the after-tax cost. At par, r_D simply equals the coupon rate.

Raising Equity Capital

How a company gets equity: angels and venture capital in rounds, then an IPO, then seasoned offerings. Every stage is a dilution question — who owns what fraction after the money comes in.

Key ideas
  • Pre-money + investment = post-money; price per share, investor ownership, and founder ownership are all computed off the share count AFTER the round.
  • IPO price by multiples: Equity Value = Earnings x (P/E) or Sales x (P/S); divide by post-IPO shares, not pre-IPO shares.
  • Auction (Dutch) IPO: sort bids high to low, cumulate demand; the clearing price is the first (highest) price where cumulative demand ≥ shares offered, and every winner pays that price.
  • Underwriter spread: fee = gross proceeds x spread; compare deals by net price per share = offer x (1 − spread). SEO sizing works backward: gross needed = net needed / (1 − spread).
  • Underpricing = (first-day close − offer) / offer; money left on the table = (close − offer) x shares sold. Winners are allocated investors and the underwriter; losers are the issuer and pre-IPO shareholders.
  • Rights offerings and fairly-priced equity issues do not transfer wealth: existing shareholders lose nothing if they exercise or sell rights, and the NPV of an equity-financed project accrues entirely to existing shares.

14.1 Equity Financing for Private Companies

  • Private companies raise equity from four sources: angel investors (wealthy individuals investing personal capital in early-stage startups, often via a convertible note or SAFE), venture capital firms (specialized funds where general partners make decisions and limited partners supply capital, charging 2% management plus 20% carried interest), institutional investors (pension funds, insurance companies, endowments, foundations investing directly or as LPs in VC funds), and corporate investors (established corporations seeking new technology, acquisition intelligence, or market positioning, e.g. Google Ventures, Intel Capital).
  • Equation 14.1: Post-money valuation = Pre-money valuation + Amount invested, where pre-money = price per share x existing shares outstanding and post-money = price per share x total shares after the investment.
  • A new investor's ownership percentage equals new shares / total shares after the investment, which is the same as the amount invested / post-money valuation.
  • U.S. venture capital funding (Figure 14.2) shows a dot-com peak around 2000 and strong acceleration after 2015.
  • VCs and angels plan for liquidity from day one; the two exit routes are acquisition (most common, can happen at any stage, shareholders receive cash and/or acquirer stock) and IPO (typically the highest-value exit but more complex and regulated); the typical VC holding period is 5-7 years before exit.

14.2 Taking Your Firm Public: The Initial Public Offering

  • Advantages of going public: greater liquidity, better access to capital, ability for early investors to diversify, enhanced credibility, and stock as currency for acquisitions. Disadvantages: dispersed ownership, public reporting and Sarbanes-Oxley compliance, loss of privacy, short-term earnings pressure, and IPO costs including the 7% underwriter fee.
  • In a primary offering the company issues new shares and keeps the proceeds (shares outstanding increase); in a secondary offering existing shareholders such as founders or VCs sell their shares and receive the proceeds (shares outstanding unchanged).
  • The underwriter is an investment bank that manages and structures the offering; the lead underwriter (bookrunner) sets pricing and allocation and coordinates a syndicate; the 7% underwriter spread on deal size is the largest single cost of going public.
  • Companies must file a registration statement with the SEC; the preliminary prospectus (red herring) is distributed during the road show and the final prospectus includes the offering price. Facebook's May 2012 IPO raised $16.0B (421M shares at $38, $104B market cap) and closed day one at $38.23.
  • Underwriters value an IPO either by discounted free cash flow (forward-looking but highly sensitive to growth assumptions) or by comparable multiples such as P/E, EV/EBITDA, and Price/Sales of recently IPO'd peers (market-based but garbage-in, garbage-out). After a 1-2 week road show, book building collects indications of interest to set the offer price and allocate shares. Offerings can be firm commitment (underwriter buys the entire issue and bears the risk), best efforts (no guarantee), or Dutch auction (all winners pay the clearing price, the lowest price at which cumulative demand covers the shares offered; Google 2004).

14.3 IPO Puzzles

  • Puzzle 1, underpricing: the average first-day U.S. IPO return is 17% (1960-2021), and some countries average over 100%; explanations include the winner's curse, underwriter incentives, lawsuit avoidance, and signaling by quality firms.
  • Companies accept leaving money on the table because the first-day pop generates publicity, satisfied investors support future fundraising, perfect pricing is difficult and overpriced IPOs invite lawsuits, and underpricing is a one-time cost that may be small relative to long-run value created.
  • Puzzle 2, hot and cold issue markets: IPO activity is highly cyclical, with hot markets showing high volume and high first-day returns and cold markets showing near-zero activity.
  • Puzzle 3, long-run underperformance: IPOs underperform the market by about 20% over the 3-5 years after the offering, attributed to market timing at high valuations, unsustainable pre-IPO earnings management, and slowing growth; first-day buyers do well while long-run buyers do poorly.
  • Puzzle 4, high issuance cost: the U.S. underwriter spread is consistently 7%, and adding 17% average underpricing brings the total cost of a typical IPO to roughly 24%, yet the 7% spread has remained stable despite competition.

14.4 SPACs: A New Way to Go Public

  • A Special Purpose Acquisition Company (blank check company) is a shell with no operations that raises money in an IPO at $10 per unit, holds the proceeds in trust, and has 18-24 months to acquire a private company (taking it public without a traditional IPO); if no deal is found it liquidates and returns $10 per share. Over 600 SPACs launched in 2020-2021.
  • The sponsor creates the SPAC and finds the target, receiving 20% of shares (the promote or founder shares) for a nominal cost (about $25K for a $100M SPAC), which incentivizes completing any deal before the deadline.
  • SPAC shareholders buy units consisting of one share plus a fraction of a warrant, and can redeem shares at about $10 plus interest before the merger vote, which protects their downside.
  • In the de-SPAC transaction the sponsor announces the target, shareholders vote and either redeem or stay invested, high redemptions reduce available capital, PIPE investors (Private Investment in Public Equity) plug the gap often at a discount, and the target becomes public through the merger.
  • The sponsor promote combined with high redemptions creates structural dilution: remaining SPAC investors can hold shares worth less than the $10 they paid before any business performance, which is the core SPAC problem.

14.5 Raising Additional Capital: The Seasoned Equity Offering

  • A cash offer (most common in the U.S.) sells new shares to public investors through underwriters in weeks rather than months; it is fast and guaranteed if underwritten but carries a 3-5% underwriter fee, a 2-3% announcement price drop, and dilutes existing holders.
  • A rights offering (common outside the U.S.) gives existing shareholders tradeable rights to buy new shares pro rata at a subscription price, usually a 15-30% discount to market; it has lower underwriting costs and no dilution for exercising shareholders, but is more complex and risks under-subscription.
  • Stock price drops about 2-3% on an SEO announcement and then partially recovers, because the offering is a negative signal that management thinks the stock is overpriced, raises dilution concerns (more shares, lower EPS), and creates a supply shock to the share float.
  • Compared with an IPO (7% fee, months of preparation, price set from scratch, large uncertainty), an SEO carries a 3-5% fee, takes weeks, is priced relative to the market, and has less uncertainty. Facebook's December 2013 SEO sold 70M shares at $55.05 ($3.85B) 18 months after its IPO.
  • Rights offering mechanics: new shares = proceeds / subscription price; rights ratio = existing shares / new shares; ex-rights price = (old market cap + new proceeds) / (old + new shares); value of one right = old price minus ex-rights price. Shareholders do not lose wealth whether they exercise or sell their rights.

Concept checks
Who wins and who loses from IPO underpricing?
Winners: investors allocated shares at the offer price (mainly the underwriter's institutional clients) and the underwriter indirectly through client goodwill. Losers: the issuer and pre-IPO shareholders, who leave (close − offer) x shares sold on the table.
Why are IPOs underpriced?
Winner's curse (uninformed investors need compensation to participate), underwriter incentives, and the publicity from a hot first day.
In an auction (Dutch) IPO, what price do the winning bidders pay?
All winners pay the single clearing price — the highest price at which cumulative demand (sorted high to low) is at least the shares offered — regardless of what they individually bid.
Why does an equity-financed positive-NPV project raise the share price only for existing shareholders?
New investors pay fair value for their shares, so the project's NPV accrues entirely to the existing shares: price = NPV / existing shares.
Does a rights offering hurt existing shareholders?
No. Whether they exercise the rights or sell them, their total wealth is unchanged; the value of one right equals the old price minus the ex-rights price.
How does a sponsor promote dilute SPAC shareholders?
The sponsor receives roughly 20% of SPAC shares for almost nothing, so value per share after the deal = deal value / (non-redeemed + sponsor + PIPE shares), which is lower than if only public shares existed.

Chapter Fifteen

Debt Financing

Debt comes in many contracts. The ones with options inside — callable, convertible, inflation-indexed — are what get tested, along with the true cost of money once fees and calls are counted.

Key ideas
  • TIPS adjust the principal with CPI; the coupon is paid on the adjusted principal, so inflation raises both principal and coupon.
  • Yield to call is just a bond YTM computed with N = periods to the FIRST call date and FV = call price instead of par.
  • Yield to worst is the lower of YTC and YTM: a premium bond (P > par) is likely to be called (YTC < YTM); a discount bond is not (YTC > YTM).
  • Call premium is the extra the issuer pays above par to call the bond: Call Price − Par Value.
  • A convertible bond is worth at least the greater of its straight-bond value and its conversion value (ratio × stock price); convert only when the stock price exceeds the conversion price.
  • A loan with an upfront fee charges interest on the FULL loan amount but you only receive the net amount, so the true rate is Interest / Net funds, above the stated rate.
  • Public debt (bonds sold under an indenture, registered with the SEC) vs private debt (term loans, private placements, bank lines); within public debt: notes/debentures are unsecured, mortgage and asset-backed bonds are secured, and seniority (senior vs subordinated) sets the payout order in bankruptcy.

Corporate Debt

  • Private debt is negotiated directly with a bank or a small group of investors, is not registered with the SEC, and is less liquid; it includes bank term loans (set repayment schedule), revolving lines of credit, syndicated bank loans (multiple banks), and private placements.
  • Public debt is sold to the general public, must be registered with the SEC via a prospectus or offering memorandum, and trades in liquid secondary markets; it includes notes (shorter maturity), debentures (unsecured), mortgage bonds (secured by real assets), asset-backed bonds (secured by specific assets), and original issue discount (OID) bonds.
  • Secured debt is backed by specific collateral while unsecured debt is backed only by the firm's general creditworthiness; unsecured holders are subordinate to secured creditors in default, and higher seniority means lower risk and a lower required yield.
  • A single bond issuance can be divided into tranches, each with different seniority, coupon, and maturity, which lets the issuer appeal to different investor groups at the same time.
  • International bonds are classified by issuer, market, and currency: domestic bonds (local issuer, local currency), foreign bonds (foreign issuer, local market, local currency; e.g., Yankee bonds in the U.S., Samurai bonds in Japan), Eurobonds (not denominated in the local currency of the country of issue), and global bonds (offered simultaneously in several markets).

Case Study: The Hertz LBO (December 2005)

  • A leveraged buyout (LBO) occurs when a group of private investors purchases all the equity of a public corporation and finances the purchase primarily with debt.
  • Hertz was purchased from Ford Motor Company by Clayton, Dubilier & Rice, Carlyle Group, and Merrill Lynch Global PE, who issued over $11 billion of new debt in multiple tranches mixing public and private debt, much of it secured by the car fleet.
  • The sample structure was $8.7B senior secured term loans/bank debt, $1.8B senior notes (public unsecured), $0.9B senior subordinated notes (public subordinated), and $0.6B mezzanine/PIK notes (private/high yield); the junk rating reflected the high leverage.

Other Types of Debt

  • U.S. Treasury securities include Treasury bills (discount securities up to 1 year, no coupon), Treasury notes (2 to 10 years, semiannual coupons), Treasury bonds (30-year "long bonds"), TIPS (principal adjusted for inflation using the CPI, with the coupon applied to the adjusted principal), and STRIPS (zero-coupon securities created by stripping coupons from Treasuries).
  • For TIPS, Adjusted Principal = Face Value × (CPI_t / CPI_issue), and each semiannual coupon = (Adjusted Principal × Coupon Rate) / 2, so even a tiny coupon rate protects purchasing power because the principal grows with inflation.
  • Municipal bonds are issued by state and local governments with interest typically exempt from federal income tax; they may be serial (portions mature each year), revenue (backed by a specific project's revenue), general obligation (backed by the issuer's taxing power), or double-barreled (backed by both).
  • Asset securitization pools income-generating assets into a security; mortgage-backed securities (MBS) are backed by a pool of mortgages and carry prepayment risk because borrowers refinance when rates fall.
  • Collateralized debt obligations (CDOs) are securities backed by other securities and played a major role in the 2008 financial crisis.

Bond Covenants

  • Covenants are restrictive clauses in a bond contract that limit the issuer from taking actions that could undercut its ability to repay the bond.
  • Positive covenants specify what the issuer must do: maintain minimum working capital, provide regular audited financial statements, maintain adequate insurance on assets, pay taxes and stay current on obligations, and keep the company in good standing.
  • Negative covenants specify what the issuer cannot do: issue additional senior debt without permission, pay dividends above a specified amount, sell or encumber major assets, merge with or acquire other companies, or restrict subsidiary dividend payments.
  • Each covenant serves a lender-protection purpose, such as ensuring liquidity, providing transparency, protecting collateral, preventing dilution of existing bondholders' claims, preserving cash for repayment, and preventing risk shifts or value shifts that harm bondholders.
  • Because covenants protect lenders, more covenants lead to lower interest rates on the debt.

Repayment Provisions: Call Provisions and Yield to Call

  • A callable bond gives the issuer the right, but not the obligation, to repurchase the bond at a pre-specified call price before maturity; the call premium equals call price minus par and typically starts above par and declines toward par over time.
  • The call protection period (typically 3 to 5 years) is an initial period during which the bond cannot be called, assuring investors of receiving coupons for a minimum period.
  • Issuers call when interest rates have fallen so they can refinance at lower rates; this costs investors, who lose the higher-coupon cash flows.
  • Yield to call (YTC) is the yield of a callable bond computed under the assumption that it will be called on the earliest call date: Price = Σ[Coupon/(1+YTC)^t] + Call Price/(1+YTC)^N, where N is the number of periods until the first call date.
  • Yield to worst = min(YTC, YTM) and is what bond traders quote; when rates have fallen and the bond trades at a premium, YTC < YTM and a call is likely, so YTC applies; when rates have risen and the bond trades at a discount, YTC > YTM and a call is unlikely, so YTM applies; at par the two are approximately equal.

Repayment Provisions: Sinking Funds

  • With a sinking fund, the company makes regular payments into a trustee-administered fund over the life of the bond, and the trustee uses the money to repurchase bonds, usually at par, either in the open market or by lottery.
  • Sinking funds reduce default risk by retiring debt gradually, provide price support in the secondary market, and demonstrate financial discipline to lenders.
  • If sinking fund payments are insufficient to retire the entire issue, the remaining principal is due at maturity as a balloon payment, creating refinancing risk that firms must plan for.

Convertible Bonds

  • A convertible bond gives the bondholder the right to convert it into a specified number of shares of the issuer's common stock; the bondholder decides and will rationally convert only if the stock price exceeds the conversion price.
  • Conversion ratio is the number of shares received on conversion; Conversion Price = Face Value ÷ Conversion Ratio (e.g., $1,000 ÷ 20 = $50 per share); Conversion Value = Conversion Ratio × Current Stock Price.
  • A straight bond is a non-callable, non-convertible bond whose value serves as the floor and benchmark for convertible bond pricing.
  • A convertible bond is always worth at least max(Straight Bond Value, Conversion Value); at low stock prices it behaves like debt (value near the straight bond floor), near the conversion price the option value is highest and the bond trades at a premium to both floor and conversion value, and at high stock prices it behaves like equity (value near conversion value) with the premium shrinking.
  • Because convertibles offer downside protection plus upside participation, investors accept a lower coupon than on comparable straight bonds.

Combining Features in Bond Design

  • A callable convertible lets the issuer force conversion by calling when the stock price is well above the conversion price; bondholders convert rather than accept the lower call price, eliminating the debt without a cash outflow.
  • Combining a sinking fund with a call feature retires debt gradually while retaining the option to accelerate retirement if rates fall, reducing lender default risk and maximizing issuer flexibility.
  • Combining covenants with call protection restricts risky issuer actions (protecting lenders) while guaranteeing investors a minimum holding period at the contracted coupon.
  • RealNetworks' 2003 convertible issue illustrates these features: $100 million, 1.25% coupon, maturing 2010, conversion ratio 163.934 shares per $1,000 bond (conversion price $6.10), callable after year 3 if the stock exceeds $7.93 for 20 of 30 days; the low coupon reflects the equity upside investors receive.

Concept checks
Why is a premium bond's yield to call lower than its yield to maturity?
When the price is above par, the investor takes a capital loss down to the call price sooner (at the call date) rather than later (at maturity), which pulls the yield down; the issuer is also likely to call because it can refinance at a lower rate, so YTW = YTC.
When does the holder of a convertible bond convert?
Only when the stock price exceeds the conversion price (Face / Conversion Ratio), so the conversion value (Ratio × Stock Price) exceeds the straight-bond value.
Why does the true rate on a loan with fees exceed the stated rate?
Interest is charged on the full loan amount, but the borrower receives only Loan × (1 − fee%); dividing the same interest by a smaller net amount gives a higher effective rate.
How does inflation affect a TIPS bond's cash flows?
The principal is scaled by CPI_t / CPI_issue, and the coupon is the stated rate applied to that adjusted principal (halved for semiannual), so both principal and coupon rise with inflation.
What is yield to worst and why does an investor use it?
YTW = min(YTC, YTM). It is the conservative yield an investor should assume on a callable bond, since the issuer will exercise the call when it benefits the issuer (and hurts the investor).

Capital Structure

Modigliani–Miller: in perfect markets, how you finance a firm doesn't change its value. Taxes make debt valuable (the interest tax shield); distress costs cap it. Everything else is arithmetic on those two ideas.

Key ideas
  • MM Proposition I (perfect markets): V_L = V_U = E + D. Firm value and WACC are set by the risk of the firm's assets, not by how they are financed. Leverage cannot make shareholders better off on its own.
  • MM Proposition II: r_E = r_U + (D/E)(r_U − r_D). Debt raises the expected return on equity only as compensation for the extra risk shareholders bear. Pretax WACC = (E/V)r_E + (D/V)r_D = r_U and stays flat as leverage changes.
  • Interest tax shield: ITS = τ_C x r_D x D each year. With taxes, V_L = V_U + PV(ITS). For permanent debt PV = τ_C x D (the interest rate cancels); for amortizing debt compute each year's shield on the beginning balance and discount at r_D.
  • After-tax WACC = (E/V)r_E + (D/V)r_D(1 − τ_C) falls as leverage rises; if pretax WACC is constant the reduction is exactly (D/V) r_D τ_C. This is WHY firms use debt.
  • Interest lowers net income by Int(1 − τ) but RAISES free cash flow by Int x τ, because FCF is unlevered (starts from EBIT) and only picks up the tax saving.
  • Tradeoff theory: V_L = V_U + PV(Tax Shield) − PV(Financial Distress Costs); optimal D* is where marginal benefit equals marginal cost. Pecking order: retained earnings, then debt, then equity as a last resort. Distress cost benchmarks: direct 3-4% of value, indirect 10-20%.

16.1 Capital Structure Choices

  • Capital structure is the relative proportions of debt, equity, and other securities a firm has outstanding; the debt-to-value ratio D/(E+D) uses market values and measures the fraction of total firm value financed by debt.
  • When choosing financing, a manager must consider fair pricing (will the market pay a reasonable price?), tax consequences (debt and equity are taxed differently), transaction costs of issuance, and whether today's decision could limit future investments.
  • Debt-to-value ratios vary widely across industries, driven by asset tangibility, cash flow stability, and growth options (Figure 16.1).
  • Capital structure also differs markedly within an industry (e.g., Intel vs. Monolithic Power), reflecting management philosophy, growth stage, and risk tolerance (Figure 16.2).

16.2 Capital Structure in Perfect Capital Markets

  • A perfect capital market has three properties: all securities are fairly priced; there are no taxes, transaction costs, or issuance costs; and investment cash flows are independent of financing decisions. In such a market, investment decisions, not financing decisions, determine firm value and the cost of capital.
  • MM Proposition I: in perfect capital markets, VL = E + D = VU, so total firm value is independent of capital structure because total cash flows to all investors are identical regardless of financing.
  • Leverage amplifies both gains and losses to equity: in the coffee shop example, the levered equity return ranges from -25% to +75% (a 100% spread) versus -10% to +40% (a 50% spread) for unlevered equity, while debt earns a flat 5%, because debt holders absorb none of the variation.
  • MM Proposition II: rE = rU + (D/E)(rU − rD). As D/E rises, rE rises proportionally as compensation for higher equity risk, while the pretax WACC = (E/(E+D))rE + (D/(E+D))rD stays flat at rU.
  • Leverage raises the expected return on equity without changing firm value or WACC; investors can also replicate any firm's leverage personally through homemade leverage.

16.3 Debt and Taxes

  • Corporations can deduct interest payments from taxable income but dividends are not deductible; this asymmetry gives debt an effective government subsidy called the interest tax shield.
  • Interest Tax Shield = τ_C x Interest Payments = τ_C x rD x D; each dollar of interest saves τ_C dollars of taxes, and the shield increases cash flows available to all investors.
  • MM with taxes (Eq. 16.5): VL = VU + PV(Interest Tax Shield), so a levered firm is worth more than an otherwise identical unlevered firm. Because the tax savings are as risky as the debt, they are discounted at the cost of debt.
  • WACC with taxes (Eqs. 16.8-16.9): rWACC = (E/(E+D))rE + (D/(E+D))rD(1 − τ_C) = Pretax WACC − rD x τ_C x D/(E+D); the after-tax cost of debt rD(1 − τ_C) is below rD, so WACC falls as leverage rises.
  • In perfect markets a bond issue has NPV = 0, but with taxes the interest tax shield makes issuing debt a positive-NPV transaction.

16.4 Costs of Bankruptcy and Financial Distress

  • More debt raises the probability of default, creating costs that offset the tax shield benefit and explain why firms do not borrow 100%.
  • Direct costs of bankruptcy include legal fees, court costs, administrative expenses, and professional advisors (lawyers, accountants, bankers); they typically run 3-4% of pre-bankruptcy market value and are higher for complex operations or many creditors. Creditors also incur costs and may wait years for payment.
  • Indirect costs of financial distress are often larger, with an estimated potential loss of 10-20% of firm value: loss of customers who need future support, loss of suppliers unwilling to extend credit, employee turnover and loss of key talent, and fire sales of assets at distressed prices.
  • Indirect costs occur before any formal bankruptcy filing; the mere threat of financial distress imposes real costs that reduce firm value.

16.5 Optimal Capital Structure: The Tradeoff Theory

  • Tradeoff Theory (Eq. 16.10): VL = VU + PV(Interest Tax Shield) − PV(Financial Distress Costs), which implies an interior optimal debt level.
  • Benefits of debt that increase with leverage: the interest tax shield (τ_C x D), lower WACC and higher firm value, improved managerial discipline, less free cash flow available for waste, and a confidence signal to investors.
  • Costs of debt that increase with leverage: higher probability of financial distress, direct bankruptcy costs (3-4% of value), indirect costs (lost customers and suppliers), agency conflicts such as excessive risk-taking, and the underinvestment problem when distressed.
  • The optimal debt level D* is where the marginal benefit of the tax shield equals the marginal PV of distress costs; firm value rises with debt (tax shield) and then falls (distress costs), peaking at D*.
  • D* is lower for firms with high distress costs, volatile cash flows, or valuable intangible assets (software, biotech) and higher for firms with low distress costs (airlines, utilities); this explains why leverage varies across industries (Figure 16.8).

16.6 Agency Costs and Information

  • Agency costs arise from conflicts of interest between stakeholders. Managerial agency costs stem from the separation of ownership and control: managers may reduce effort, spend excessively on perks, or engage in empire building. Debt helps because required interest payments reduce free cash flow available to waste, concentrated ownership with debt improves monitoring, and leverage disciplines managers.
  • Equity-debt holder conflicts arise when a firm is near distress: excessive risk-taking (shareholders 'bet the farm' on negative-NPV projects), underinvestment (declining positive-NPV projects whose benefits go mainly to creditors), and cash-out (distributing assets to shareholders before creditors take over). These actions destroy total firm value while transferring wealth from creditors to shareholders.
  • Figure 16.9: too little leverage (D < D*) means lost tax benefits, excessive perks, empire-building, and squandered free cash flow; too much leverage (D > D*) means excess interest burden, financial distress costs, excessive risk-taking, and underinvestment. D* balances all benefits and costs of leverage and maximizes firm value.
  • Asymmetric information (managers know more about future cash flows than investors) shapes financing: leverage credibly signals confidence (signaling theory), managers issue equity when they believe the stock is overvalued and use debt or retained earnings when it is undervalued (market timing), and investors therefore discount new equity issues (adverse selection, the lemons problem).
  • Pecking Order Hypothesis (Myers & Majluf): managers prefer retained earnings first (no new issue), then debt (limited information disclosure), and equity only as a last resort, because information asymmetry makes external financing, especially equity, expensive.

16.7 Putting It All Together

  • Use the interest tax shield if the firm has consistent taxable income; stable firms such as utilities benefit more than firms with volatile earnings.
  • Balance the tax benefits of debt against the costs of financial distress; firms with volatile cash flows or valuable intangible assets should use less debt.
  • Consider short-term debt for external financing when agency costs are significant, since it limits managerial flexibility and reduces wasteful spending; increase leverage to credibly signal confidence in the firm's ability to meet obligations.
  • Be mindful of adverse selection (investors know managers have an incentive to issue overpriced securities, so equity issues are discounted) and follow the pecking order: retained earnings, then debt, then equity.
  • Do not change the capital structure unless it departs significantly from the optimal level, because transaction costs and market disruption make frequent changes costly.

Appendix: The Bankruptcy Code (Chapter 7 vs. Chapter 11)

  • Chapter 7 is liquidation: a trustee oversees the sale of all assets, proceeds go to creditors by priority (secured creditors first, equity holders last and often receiving nothing), and the firm ceases to exist; it is used when reorganization is not feasible.
  • Chapter 11 is reorganization: existing management proposes a plan, operations continue during restructuring, the plan specifies treatment of each creditor class, creditors vote and the court must confirm; if no acceptable plan emerges, the court may force Chapter 7 liquidation.
  • The goal of Chapter 11 is to preserve going-concern value; creditors prefer Chapter 11 when going-concern value exceeds liquidation value.

Concept checks
Does swapping equity for debt lower a firm's overall cost of capital in perfect markets?
No. WACC is determined by the risk of the firm's assets, not how they are financed (MM). More debt makes both debt and equity riskier; the average stays put. Pretax WACC = r_U regardless of leverage.
A manager argues the firm should pick the capital structure with the highest expected stock return. Is that right?
Wrong. The higher expected return is compensation for the higher risk leverage puts on shareholders. Firm value and WACC are unchanged (MM I), so shareholders are no better off. Maximize firm value, not stock return.
Why does the founder's stake have the same value whether the $2M is raised with all equity or with $1M debt plus $1M equity?
MM I: total firm value is the same ($4.0M) under either structure. With $1M of debt, equity is worth $3.0M and selling one-third of it raises the needed $1M, leaving the founder with 2/3 x 3.0 = $2.0M, identical to 50% x 4.0 = $2.0M. The success/failure payoff is a distractor.
If interest expense rises, why does net income fall but free cash flow rise?
NI is after interest, so it falls by Int(1 − τ). FCF is unlevered (it starts from EBIT), so interest only shows up through the tax saving: FCF rises by Int x τ.
Why does the interest rate not matter for the PV of the tax shield on permanent debt?
The annual shield τ r_D D is a perpetuity discounted at r_D, so PV = τ r_D D / r_D = τ D. Only τ and D matter.
Why do firms use debt, given MM?
With corporate taxes, interest is deductible, creating an interest tax shield. V_L = V_U + PV(ITS), and after-tax WACC = (E/V)r_E + (D/V)r_D(1 − τ_C) falls as leverage rises.
What limits how much debt a firm should take on?
Tradeoff theory: V_L = V_U + PV(Tax Shield) − PV(Financial Distress Costs). The optimal D* is where the marginal benefit of the shield equals the marginal cost of distress. Benchmarks: direct distress costs 3-4% of value, indirect 10-20%.
What is the pecking order of financing?
Retained earnings first, then debt, then equity as a last resort.
How can you check an income-statement tax shield calculation?
NI + Int − EBIT(1 − τ) = ITS. Total cash to debt + equity holders is higher by exactly the shield (Pelamed: 128.76 − 116.01 = 12.75).
How do you read financial distress costs off a leveraged recap announcement?
If taxes were the only imperfection the price would rise by τ D / shares. If it rises less, PV(distress) = τ D − delta P x shares (Hawar: 4.0 − 2.5 = $1.5M).

Estimating Coca-Cola's Beta

Textbook betas are quoted; this one is estimated. Sixty months of Coca-Cola, S&P 500, and T-bill prices pulled from Yahoo Finance, converted to excess returns, and regressed — the CAPM as a line of best fit.

Method
  • Monthly adjusted closes for KO, SPY (market proxy), and BIL (1–3 month T-bill ETF, the risk-free proxy), September 2021 – August 2026.
  • Simple monthly returns in percent, then excess returns: KO − BIL and SPY − BIL. The CAPM is a statement about excess returns, so the regression has to be too.
  • Ordinary least squares with a constant: KO_rp = α + β · SPY_rp + ε. The slope is beta; the intercept is alpha.
Code
import pandas as pd
import yfinance as yf
import statsmodels.api as sm

tix = 'KO'
Prices = yf.download(['SPY', 'BIL', tix], start='2021-08-01', end='2026-08-31',
                     interval='1mo', multi_level_index=False, auto_adjust=False)['Adj Close']

Rets = 100 * Prices.pct_change().dropna()        # monthly returns, in percent
Rets['KO_rp']  = Rets['KO']  - Rets['BIL']        # excess return on KO
Rets['SPY_rp'] = Rets['SPY'] - Rets['BIL']        # market risk premium

X = sm.add_constant(Rets['SPY_rp'])               # constant → alpha
capm = sm.OLS(Rets['KO_rp'], X).fit()
print('Alpha for KO:', round(capm.params['const'], 4))
print('Beta for KO: ', round(capm.params['SPY_rp'], 4))

Reading it
  • β ≈ 0.34. A 1% move in the market's excess return has come with roughly a 0.34% move in Coca-Cola's. A defensive, low-systematic-risk stock — consistent with a consumer staple and with published estimates in the 0.4–0.6 range once you account for the five-year window.
  • α ≈ 0.55% per month, not significant (p = 0.36). The interval comfortably includes zero, so there is no evidence KO earned abnormal returns beyond what the CAPM predicts.
  • R² ≈ 0.10. Only about a tenth of KO's monthly variation is explained by the market; the rest is firm-specific — exactly the risk Chapter 11 says a diversified investor isn't paid to bear.
  • Why it matters for Chapter 13: plug β into the CAPM and you have KO's cost of equity — the input WACC needs. The regression is the step the textbook skips.

Formula Sheet

The one-page sheet, as it was written out for the final: Chapters 14–16 first (about 70% of the exam), then the cumulative Chapters 11–13.

Ch 14 · Raising equity

  • Post-money = Pre-money + Investment
  • Pre = price × existing sh · Post = price × sh after
  • Investor % = new sh / total after = Investment / Post
  • Shares for stake f: x = existing × f / (1 − f)
  • IPO price = (Earnings × P/E) / shares after
  • Auction IPO: sort bids high→low, cumulate; clearing price = lowest winning bid (first row where cumulative ≥ offered). All winners pay it.
  • Net price/sh = Offer × (1 − spread)
  • Underpricing = (close − offer) / offer · $ on table = (close − offer) × sh
  • SEO: gross = net / (1 − spread); shares = gross / price
  • Rights: ex-rights P = (old cap + proceeds) / total sh; right = P₀ − P_ex

Ch 15 · Debt financing

  • TIPS principal = Face × CPI_t / CPI_issue · coupon = principal × rate / 2
  • YTC: N = periods to first call · PMT = coupon · PV = −price · FV = call price → I/Y
  • YTW = min(YTC, YTM) — premium bond → call likely
  • Call premium = call price − par
  • Conversion price = Face / ratio · conversion value = ratio × stock price
  • True loan rate = interest / [loan × (1 − fee)]

Ch 16 · Capital structure

  • D/V = D / (D + E) · D/E = x → D/V = x / (1 + x)
  • MM I: V_L = V_U in perfect markets
  • MM II: r_E = r_U + (D/E)(r_U − r_D)
  • Pretax WACC = (E/V) r_E + (D/V) r_D = r_U
  • ITS = τ r_D D · permanent: PV = τD · amortizing: npv(r_D, 0, {balances}) × r_D × τ
  • V_U = FCF / r_U · V_L = V_U + PV(ITS)
  • WACC = (E/V) r_E + (D/V) r_D (1 − τ) = r_U − (D/V) τ r_D
  • Recap: P_new = P₀ + τD / shares · PV(distress) = τD − ΔP × shares
  • Tradeoff: V_L = V_U + PV(ITS) − PV(distress) · pecking order: retained earnings → debt → equity

Ch 11 · Risk and return

  • R = (Div₁ + P₁ − P₀) / P₀ = dividend yield + capital-gain rate
  • 1 + R_ann = (1 + R₁)(1 + R₂)…(1 + Rₙ)
  • Arithmetic = Σ R_t / T · Geometric = [Π(1 + R_t)]^(1/T) − 1 (≤ arithmetic)
  • Var = Σ(R_t − R̄)² / (T − 1) · SD = √Var · SE = SD / √T
  • 95% interval = R̄ ± 2 SD
  • Independent risks: SD(avg of n) = SD(one) / √n

Ch 12 · Systematic risk & CAPM

  • w_i = value_i / total · R_P = Σ w_i R_i (beginning-of-period weights)
  • Var(R_P) = w₁²SD₁² + w₂²SD₂² + 2 w₁ w₂ Corr SD₁ SD₂
  • Cov = Corr × SD₁ × SD₂ · β_i = Cov(R_i, R_mkt) / Var(R_mkt) = Corr × SD_i / SD_mkt
  • E[R_i] = r_f + β_i (E[R_mkt] − r_f) · β_P = Σ w_i β_i
  • MRP = market return − r_f. Subtract first if you are given the return.

Ch 13 · Cost of capital

  • E = price × shares · V = E + P + D — market values, never book
  • WACC = (E/V) r_E + (P/V) r_P + (D/V) r_D (1 − τ)
  • r_P = Div_pfd / P_pfd · CAPM r_E = r_f + β MRP · CDGM r_E = Div₁ / P₀ + g
  • Net debt = D − Cash · WACC = r_E (E/EV) + r_D (1 − τ)(Net debt / EV)
  • V₀ = FCF₁ / (WACC − g) · NPV = −I₀ + V₀ − issuance costs
  • FCF = EBIT (1 − τ) + Dep − CapEx − ΔNWC

Quick checks

  • A portfolio return lies between its best and worst holding.
  • Geometric ≤ arithmetic. Variance divides by T − 1.
  • Discount bond → YTM above coupon; premium → below.
  • The tax shield is on debt only — never preferred or equity.
  • Calculator: END not BGN · P/Y = 1 · CLR TVM first.
